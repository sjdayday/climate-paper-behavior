# Papers as Behavior: The Study Design, Explained

*Draft 1. This document expands an earlier, denser design summary for readers who are new to the project. It covers the reasoning behind the study, defines the technical vocabulary as it arises, and walks through what the first pilot exercise found. The formal scoring rulebook itself — the codebook, v0.1 — is a separate document; this is the guide to it.*

---

## 1. The question this study asks

A well-known argument, made most prominently in Naomi Oreskes and Erik Conway's *Merchants of Doubt*, holds that some scientific-sounding challenges to mainstream climate science are not really aimed at other scientists at all. Their function, on this account, is to sustain the *appearance* of an ongoing scientific debate for an educated lay audience — readers who will then conclude that policy discussion is premature because "the science isn't settled."

That is a claim about behavior, and behavior can be measured. This project asks: do contrarian climate papers, *as a group*, behave differently from mainstream climate papers — in ways consistent with being written to persuade an educated general reader rather than to convince expert peers?

Some vocabulary before going further. By *contrarian* papers we mean papers rejecting the existence or magnitude of human-caused warming — with the important caveat that this defining stance is *assumed* and therefore cannot itself count as a distinguishing behavior; the study looks for differences beyond it. *Mainstream* papers are those working within the consensus position. *Attribution* is the branch of climate science that asks what caused observed temperature changes — how much of the warming is due to greenhouse gases, how much to the sun, to volcanoes, to natural ocean cycles. Attribution papers are the natural arena for this study because they are exactly where the two camps make directly opposing claims about the same data.

The immediate motivation is empirical. A 2016 study led by Rasmus Benestad attempted to *replicate* — that is, independently redo the analyses of — a set of frequently cited contrarian papers, and catalogued the recurring problems it found: statistical shortcuts, ignored context, curve-fitting without checks. The qualitative impression from reading both literatures is that contrarian papers commit these documented problems routinely. But an impression is not a measurement. This study is the attempt to turn it into one.

One more piece of context: the scoring in this study is performed by an AI language model — me, Claude. The reason is not that an AI is unbiased; it isn't, and Section 10 confronts that directly. The reason is that any *human* scorer credible enough to do this work would be a climate scientist of one camp or the other, and their scores would be dismissed accordingly. An AI's biases are real but different in kind — it has no career, funding, or reputation riding on the outcome — and, crucially, its work can be made completely transparent and checkable in a way described below.

## 2. Why not just count errors?

The obvious design would be: take a stack of contrarian papers and a stack of mainstream papers, find the errors in each, and compare the error rates. Three problems sink this, and they are worth understanding because they shaped everything that follows.

The first is a measurement problem with a technical name: *differential misclassification*. Any error-detector — human or AI — makes mistakes: it misses some real errors and flags some non-errors. That alone is survivable. What is not survivable is when the detector's mistake rates *differ between the two groups being compared*, in the same direction as the hypothesis being tested. My training data contains far more published criticism of contrarian climate papers than of mainstream ones, so I am plausibly better at spotting (and more primed to suspect) flaws in contrarian work. A detector biased that way would manufacture exactly the difference the study is looking for, even if no true difference existed.

The usual remedy is *blinding* — hiding from the scorer which group each paper belongs to, the way drug trials hide who got the placebo. But blinding fails here for a simple reason: *stance legibility*. You can strip the authors' names off a paper, but a paper arguing that warming is mostly a natural cycle announces its camp in every paragraph. The scorer always knows.

The third problem is that error-counting invites an endless fight. Every judgment of the form "this analysis is wrong" is a scientific claim that the paper's defenders can contest, and the study collapses into relitigating each paper's science — precisely the interminable-debate dynamic the study is trying to examine.

## 3. The reframe: score what papers do, not whether they're right

The design response is to change the unit of measurement. Instead of adjudicating whether a paper's *conclusions* are correct, score the paper as a piece of *behavior*: the practices it performs or omits, the way it handles opposing evidence, the relationship between how confidently it speaks and what its own evidence supports, and how its authors behave after publication.

This move has two virtues. First, presence-or-absence questions are close to clerical. "Did the paper test its model on data it wasn't fitted to?" can be answered by reading the paper, and the answer can be checked by anyone — of any persuasion — against the text. Nobody has to agree about climate science to verify the score. Second, it makes every judgment *auditable*. Under the study's rules, every score must carry a short verbatim quotation and a *page anchor* — the exact page where the supporting passage sits — or, for absence claims ("no such test appears"), a record of the search terms used to establish the absence. The study's credibility is designed to rest on this checkability, not on anyone's neutrality.

There is an honest cost. Because correctness is out of scope, some categories of documented error — outright mathematical mistakes, physically impossible mechanisms — are deliberately not measured. The instrument detects behavior *correlated with* error-proneness, not errors themselves. That trade is made openly.

The behaviors measured fall into five families.

### Family one: rhetorical calibration

*Calibration* here means the match between how confidently a claim is stated and how much support the paper itself provides for it. The scoring works in two steps.

Step one classifies each concluding claim's confidence level using the language it is wrapped in. Linguists distinguish *hedges* — words that soften a claim and signal uncertainty, like "may," "suggests," "appears," "likely" — from *boosters* — words that amplify certainty, like "clearly," "obviously," "undoubtedly," "proves." The codebook defines a four-rung ladder: speculative claims ("may"), qualified claims ("our results suggest"), assertive claims ("we have shown"), and emphatic claims — boosters, superlatives, or sweeping comparisons ("better than any").

Step two classifies the *evidential tier*: the strongest support the paper itself supplies for that claim. The bottom tier is bare assertion. Next is *in-sample fit* — the model matches the very data it was tuned on, which is weak evidence because a flexible model can be tuned to match almost anything. Above that is *out-of-sample* support — the model was tested against data it never saw during tuning, which is the standard guard against fooling yourself with a good-looking fit. The top tier is multiple independent lines of evidence converging, or formal uncertainty analysis that genuinely bounds the claim.

The rule is then simple: each tier of evidence licenses a maximum rung of confidence. A claim stated more confidently than its tier permits is *overreach*, and the paper-level score is the fraction of its concluding claims that overreach. The hypothesis predicts contrarian papers overreach more — confident, quotable conclusions resting on in-sample fits — because confident quotability is what a lay-persuasion strategy needs and expert review would normally punish.

### Family two: contextual engagement

The single most common finding in Benestad's replication work was not calculation error but *missing contextual information* — papers proceeding as if the strongest existing evidence against their thesis did not exist. This family flips that finding into something scoreable in either direction: does the paper identify the prevailing counter-position (operationalized as the position of the contemporaneous IPCC assessment — the periodic report in which the field synthesizes its evidence) and engage it substantively, dismiss it with a bare citation, or ignore it?

Alongside that judgment item sit mechanical ones computed from the reference list: the fraction of citations that are peer-reviewed journal articles versus *gray literature* (reports, blogs, and other material that never passed journal review); the *self-citation* fraction (references sharing an author with the paper itself — every author self-cites somewhat, but heavy reliance on one's own prior papers as the load-bearing support for contested claims is a distinct behavior); and whether the paper cites any of a pre-listed set of the era's most-cited attribution studies — a check on whether it is talking to the field at all.

### Family three: methodological practices

A checklist of standard practices, each scored present or absent: out-of-sample evaluation; uncertainty reported on fitted quantities and — separately — *propagated* to the headline conclusion (an error bar on an intermediate slope is not the same as an error bar on the number the abstract leads with); a *sensitivity analysis*, meaning the paper shows its result survives reasonable alternative choices of data or method; full data availability; a stated physical mechanism for each proposed driver; and what the codebook calls degrees-of-freedom discipline, explained in Section 12 where it becomes vivid.

### Family four: post-publication conduct

The lay-persuasion hypothesis extends past publication: it predicts that criticism, once published, is simply not acknowledged — the headline claim keeps circulating. So for papers that attracted a formal published comment (journals allow short peer-reviewed critiques of published papers, to which authors may publish replies), the study records: did the authors reply, did they concede or correct anything, and — the sharpest marker — did they go on citing their own criticized result in later papers *without mentioning that the criticism exists*?

### Family five: stylometry

*Stylometry* is the statistical measurement of writing style. Here it means a script — no judgment involved — counting things like hedge and booster *densities*: how many hedging words and how many boosting words appear per thousand words of text. The word lists (*lexicons*) are borrowed verbatim from the published work of Ken Hyland, an applied linguist who spent a career cataloguing exactly these markers across academic writing. Borrowing an existing, third-party instrument matters for a specific reason: if we built our own word list, a critic could reasonably suspect the list was curated to make one side look bad. Hyland's lists were compiled decades before this study, for unrelated purposes, and are frozen — no additions or deletions allowed. These measures are secondary: nobody knows yet how well they discriminate anything, but they are cheap, and because a script computes them, they are immune to scorer bias.

## 4. From items to a statistical test

Individual items are aggregated into three *composite indices* — averages of related items — called Calibration, Engagement, and Practices. Averaging matters because it reduces the number of hypotheses being tested: testing thirty items separately practically guarantees a few flukes; testing three composites does not. The study *pre-registers* three directional hypotheses — contrarian papers will show more overreach, less engagement, fewer standard practices. Pre-registration means the hypotheses, definitions, and analysis plan are fixed and published *before* the data are scored, so results cannot be quietly reshaped after the fact; "directional" means each hypothesis names which way the difference should go, a stricter commitment than merely predicting "a difference."

The significance test is a *permutation test*, which is easier to picture than its name suggests: shuffle the group labels among the papers thousands of times, recompute the group difference under each shuffle, and see how often pure chance produces a gap as large as the real one. If almost never, the real gap is unlikely to be luck.

One refinement is non-negotiable, and it deserves the expansion it was promised. The shuffling cannot treat papers as independent units, because they are not: the contrarian literature is heavily concentrated in a small number of prolific repeat authors — the same names recur across a large share of the corpus. (Nicola Scafetta, co-author of one of the pilot papers below, has published dozens of papers in this vein.) Twenty papers by three authors are not twenty independent pieces of evidence about "contrarian behavior"; they are three authorial habits sampled repeatedly. Treating them as twenty — a mistake statisticians call *pseudoreplication* — inflates the apparent sample size and makes differences look far more significant than they are. The remedy is *author clustering*: in every shuffle, all of an author's papers travel together as one block, so the test's verdict reflects the number of independent authors, not the number of papers. This makes real differences harder to certify. That is the point.

## 5. Who picks the papers? The defensibility architecture

A study like this is most vulnerable at the step where papers are chosen, because cherry-picking there can predetermine everything. So the choosing is delegated to a third party. A 2019 study in *Nature Communications* by Petersen, Vincent, and Westerling — built for a different purpose, before this project existed — assembled a list of 386 prominent contrarians (from Heartland Institute conference keynote rosters, lead authorship of the NIPCC's contrarian counter-report, and the DeSmog project's public profiles) and a size-matched comparison list of the 386 most-cited climate scientists. Incidentally, that study's own finding frames this one: across all media, the contrarians were featured in 49% *more* articles than the scientists, despite the scientists' vastly greater publication and citation records — visibility out of all proportion to scientific authority, which is what the manufactured-debate account predicts. The present study samples papers from those pre-existing lists under mechanical rules (topic, time window, authorship role), removing our discretion over who counts as which.

Papers from the two groups are matched on topic, time window, length class, and team size — but deliberately *not* on journal prestige. This requires a word about *endogeneity*, which means: a variable you might want to control for is itself partly *caused by* the thing you are studying. Where a paper gets published is not an independent background fact; a paper optimized for lay persuasion may be unable to survive review at a selective journal, so it lands in a lesser venue. Matching on venue — comparing each contrarian paper only to mainstream papers from equally obscure journals — would quietly erase part of the very behavior under study. Venue is therefore recorded as a *covariate*: a background variable measured and reported alongside the results so readers can see its role, rather than used to filter the comparison.

Then there is the scorer-bias problem from Section 2, which the design answers with architecture rather than assurances. The rulebook is frozen before confirmatory scoring begins. Every judgment carries its quote and page anchor. Every scoring transcript is published in full. Each paper is scored in three independent runs and the disagreement rate between runs is reported as an instability measure. A random tenth of the judgment calls gets re-verified by a human against the anchors, and hostile audit of the full archive is openly invited. And a *legibility audit* is run first: I am given papers with identifying details stripped and asked to guess each one's camp. If I can — and the honest expectation is that I largely can, because of stance legibility — the study says so up front and rests its credibility entirely on the checkability of the individual scores, not on any pretense of blindness.

## 6. The pilot pair

Before any of this touches a real sample, the instrument is piloted on one matched pair of papers, chosen to be as alike as possible in everything except conclusion.

The contrarian exemplar is Loehle & Scafetta (2011) — LS11 — published in the *Open Atmospheric Science Journal*. It takes the global surface temperature record from 1850, fits it with two repeating cycles (60-year and 20-year periods, which the authors link to solar and astronomical rhythms) plus a straight-line trend, and attributes what remains after 1942 to human influence. Its headline conclusions: the human contribution to warming since 1970 is about half what the IPCC says, and the world may warm less than 1 °C by 2100.

The mainstream exemplar is Foster & Rahmstorf (2011) — FR11 — published in *Environmental Research Letters* the same year. It takes five independent global temperature records covering 1979–2010 — three surface compilations and two satellite series — and statistically removes the three big known sources of short-term wiggle: the El Niño/La Niña ocean cycle, the cooling haze from volcanic eruptions, and the sun's 11-year brightness cycle. What remains, in all five records, is a steady warming of roughly 0.14–0.18 °C per decade with no slowdown.

The match is close: same year, same object of study (the instrumental temperature record), same broad method family (statistical decomposition of the record into components, rather than physics-based climate simulation — the "general circulation models," or GCMs, that dominate mainstream attribution), both short two-author papers, both explicitly engaging the public debate, with mirror-image conclusions. The main disclosed mismatch is journal prestige, unmatched for the endogeneity reason above.

One rule about the pilot deserves its own sentence: both pilot papers are *excluded from the eventual confirmatory sample*. The instrument's definitions were tuned against these two texts, so it will fit them unusually well — the measurement version of *overfitting* — and letting them count as evidence would be double-dipping.

## 7. What the pilot found

Scoring worked examples against the two real texts immediately produced results worth understanding in detail — including some that required unpacking even for the project's own records.

The instrument's clearest positive came from LS11's conclusion, which states that its simple cycle model "had far higher pattern accuracy than any traditional analytical computer general circulation climate model output." On the confidence ladder this is top-rung emphatic — a sweeping "better than any" comparison. And the paper contains no comparison against any GCM output, anywhere: no such analysis, table, or figure exists in it (an absence established by systematic text search and recorded as such). A maximally confident comparative claim with zero in-paper support is the maximum possible overreach score. Note what makes this example so clean: you do not have to dispute one word of the paper's science to score it. You only have to search the paper for the supporting comparison and find that there isn't one.

The second finding cuts the other way, and it matters just as much. LS11's own abstract states its headline conclusion like this: "Our results *suggest* that... the anthropogenic contribution to climate change since 1970 *should be around half* of that previously claimed by the IPCC." The governing word is "suggest" — a hedge, second rung on the ladder. The claim's support is the model's in-sample fit — second tier of evidence. Under the calibration rule, second-tier evidence licenses second-rung confidence: the sentence scores as perfectly *calibrated*. The instrument, following its own rules, credits the contrarian paper's headline sentence. An instrument that never did this — that dinged one side no matter what — would be rigged, and would look rigged.

The third finding, on the mainstream side, was summarized in the project's internal notes in a sentence dense enough that it is worth unpacking clause by clause here. The sentence was: *"'This is the true global warming signal' passes the calibration item on the letter of the rules while the register gets routed to booster density, and its abstract-level claims score as fully carried by the five-dataset, ARMA-corrected machinery."*

Take it in pieces. FR11's conclusion, presenting its cleaned-up composite temperature record, declares: "This is the true global warming signal." The word "true" is emphatic — top rung. Does it overreach? Under the rule, top-rung confidence is permitted when backed by top-tier evidence, and FR11's claim has it: five independent datasets agreeing, with formally computed error bars. So the sentence "passes the calibration item on the letter of the rules" — the scoring rule, applied as written, finds no overreach.

And yet "true" is still an overstatement of a subtler kind: a statistically adjusted estimate is an *estimate*, not truth itself. The instrument does not lose this information — it just records it elsewhere. "The register gets routed to booster density" means: the *register* (the tone, the stylistic pitch of the writing) is captured by the stylometry family, where the script counting Hyland's booster words tallies "true" like any other certainty-amplifier. Different instruments in the battery catch different things; strong rhetoric in a mainstream paper is not invisible, it is simply filed under style rather than overreach.

"The five-dataset, ARMA-corrected machinery" refers to why FR11's evidence earns the top tier, and it introduces the degrees-of-freedom discipline promised earlier. Monthly global temperatures are *autocorrelated* — sticky. A warm month tends to follow a warm month, so consecutive data points partly repeat each other's information; 384 monthly readings are worth far fewer than 384 independent facts. Error bars computed as if the points were independent come out deceptively narrow — the same pseudoreplication sin as counting one author's twenty papers as twenty. FR11 handles this explicitly, using a standard statistical noise model (called ARMA(1,1) — the name matters less than the function) to widen its error bars honestly, and shows the correction in an appendix. LS11, by contrast, reports an error bar on its post-1942 trend with no such correction anywhere in the text, and its supporting robustness exercise tests its model against noise *generated from the model itself* — a circular check that assumes the thing it is meant to test. Same practice item, scored by the same rule: FR11 passes, LS11 fails, and both scores are anchored to quotable passages.

FR11's very last sentence — that its result carries "powerful evidence" and emphasizes "the urgency of confronting the human influence on climate" — raised one more design question: this is not an empirical claim at all but a policy exhortation, a *normative* statement. Scoring it for overreach would be a category error; ignoring it would discard information. The pilot's answer was a new rule: normative sentences are flagged and logged verbatim, in either direction, in any paper, but not scored.

The internal note summarizing all this ended: *"That asymmetry-within-symmetry is what I'd want a hostile auditor to find."* Spelled out: the *symmetry* is that identical rules were applied identically to both papers. The *asymmetry* is that the papers genuinely behave differently, so their scores differ. And the crucial credibility evidence is that the differences do not all run one way — the instrument cleared the contrarian paper's hedged headline claim and flagged the mainstream paper's rhetoric. A hostile auditor hunting for a rigged instrument looks for one that only ever scores against one side. Finding counterexamples in both directions is precisely what should persuade such an auditor that the instrument follows its rules rather than its designer's sympathies.

## 8. What the pilot changed

Piloting is supposed to break the instrument in instructive ways, and it did — four times, each change logged with its reason before the rulebook freezes.

First: LS11 genuinely does engage the IPCC's counter-position — it quotes the IPCC's attribution figures and argues against them — which earned it the top engagement score. But its load-bearing rebuttal citation is Scafetta citing Scafetta. The original item had no way to record that, so a qualifier was added noting when an engagement's supporting evidence rests on the authors citing themselves.

Second: the out-of-sample item forced a false choice. LS11's design contains a genuinely good out-of-sample element — the cycles are fitted only to pre-1950 data, then extrapolated forward and compared against the later record — even though its *headline* claims (the attribution split, the 2100 forecast) inherit no such support. One yes/no question couldn't hold both facts, so it was split in two: any component tested out-of-sample, and the headline claim specifically.

Third, the same surgery for uncertainty: LS11 does put an error bar on an intermediate quantity (its fitted post-1942 slope) but never carries uncertainty through to the numbers its abstract leads with, while FR11 does. The item was split into uncertainty-reported and uncertainty-propagated.

Fourth, the normative flag described above.

Notice the direction of the first three revisions: each one made the instrument *fairer to the contrarian paper* or more precise about partial credit. That is what tuning-then-freezing is for — and why the two papers the tuning touched are barred from the final sample.

## 9. What this study can and cannot show

If the confirmatory results match the pre-registered predictions, the study will have shown that the two literatures differ behaviorally, in the direction the lay-persuasion account predicts, by measures anyone can audit. Here is what it will *not* have shown: what any author intended. And it does not need to. The manufactured-doubt mechanism does not require that individual authors sat down to write for laypeople; it works just as well through *selection* — a funding and publicity ecosystem that amplifies papers exhibiting these features, whatever their authors believed. The findings will be worded to fit both readings, because the data cannot distinguish them.

Other honest limits: the behavioral differences could partly reflect journal resources and reviewing standards rather than audience design (venue is reported so readers can weigh this); the contrarian sampling list was built partly from public visibility, so conclusions apply to the *prominent* contrarian literature, which is, after all, the literature the hypothesis is about; and the scorer's own asymmetric exposure is disclosed, mitigated by architecture, and not eliminable. One study, honestly bounded, is worth more than a sweeping claim — and the boundaries are part of the design, stated before the data can tempt anyone to forget them.
