ABSTRACT
We analyze five prominent time series of global temperature (over land and ocean) for their common time interval since 1979: three surface temperature records (from NASA/GISS, NOAA/NCDC and HadCRU) and two lower-troposphere (LT) temperature records based on satellite microwave sensors (from RSS and UAH). All five series show consistent global warming trends ranging from 0.014 to 0.018 K yr-1. When the data are adjusted to remove the estimated impact of known factors on short-term temperature variations (El Nino/southern oscillation, volcanic aerosols and solar variability), the global warming signal becomes even more evident as noise is reduced. Lower-troposphere temperature responds more strongly to El Nino/southern oscillation and to volcanic forcing than surface temperature data. The adjusted data show warming at very similar rates to the unadjusted data, with smaller probable errors, and the warming rate is steady over the whole time interval. In all adjusted series, the two hottest years are 2009 and 2010.

1. INTRODUCTION
The evolution of global surface temperature and that of the lower troposphere over the last three decades is of exceptional interest, both because of its relevance for understanding anthropogenic global warming and because some public (and to a much lesser extent, scientific) debate has arisen over discrepancies between the different global temperature records, and over the exact magnitude of, and possible recent changes in, warming rates (Peterson and Baringer 2009). The nature and causes of short-term variability in global temperature, extending over a few years, are also of great interest in their own right.
It is well established that certain exogenous factors have a detectable influence on global temperature, and are responsible for some of the observed short-term variation. Chief among these are the El Nino/southern oscillation (ENSO) phenomenon, volcanic eruptions, and variations in solar output. Lean and Rind (2008) estimated the influence of these factors, as well as that of anthropogenic forcing, by multiple regression of temperature against ENSO, volcanic influence, total solar irradiance (TSI) and anthropogenic forcing; they also analyzed the geographic distribution of the temperature response. In a follow-up paper (Lean and Rind 2009) they applied their results to discuss the expected climate evolution over the coming two decades. Our regression analysis uses a similar approach to Lean and Rind, but is applied here to compare five different global temperature data sets over the time span for which all five records are available, in order to characterize their differences and similarities, and to remove an estimate of the influence of exogenous factors for which the impact on global temperature is known with some confidence. We do not include the anthropogenic forcing in our regression because over the brief 32-year period it is very nearly linear in time, so its influence cannot easily be statistically distinguished from any other source of linear trend, and because unlike the natural factors named above, its physical impact on the climate system cannot be considered to be known well from data independent of the global temperature series itself. Hence our approach is to remove from the temperature record the estimated influence of the known natural factors, in order to isolate the remaining global warming trend, whatever its cause.
We study the three main surface temperature records with global coverage, and the two main satellite-based lower-troposphere records. Surface temperature data are taken from NASA Goddard Institute for Space Studies (GISS; Hansen et al 2010), the National Climatic Data Center of the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA/NCDC; Smith et al 2008), and from the Hadley Centre/Climate Research Unit in the UK (HadCRU; Brohan et al 2006). Lower-troposphere data are from Remote Sensing Systems (RSS; Mears and Wentz 2009) and the University of Alabama at Huntsville (UAH; Christy et al 2000). Clearly, no single data record, surface or satellite, is free of possible error, and each is based on limited and imperfect sampling of the Earth, so the true evolution of global temperature (surface and LT) is not known with certainty. However, each record represents an excellent effort at estimation, applying different approaches to the problem, so the differences between them give some clue to the uncertainties involved, and their commonality gives strong clues about the true changes.

2. DATA AND METHODS
For ENSO we use the multivariate El Nino index (MEI; Wolter and Timlin 1993, 1998). For volcanic influence we use aerosol optical thickness data (AOD; Sato et al 1993). For solar irradiance we use TSI from Frohlich (2006). All temperature data are monthly averages, expressed as anomalies. The temperature data cover different time spans: surface temperature records begin as early as 1850 (HadCRU), while the satellite records begin in 1979, so 1979-2010 is the period of overlap for all five records, and it is the time span analyzed in this work.
We approximate the influence of exogenous factors as a linear (in the regression sense) combination of the ENSO, volcanic and solar variables, together with a linear time trend, allowing for a lag in the temperature response to each factor. Each of the lags was allowed to vary from 0 to 24 months, and for each of the five temperature records we selected the combination of lags which gave the best fit to the data as indicated by the value of the multiple regression correlation coefficient R.
The two temperature variables that are the object of study, i.e., surface temperature and lower-troposphere temperature, exhibit an annual cycle in their response to exogenous factors; in fact for some of the data sets, the annual cycle in temperature during the time span under analysis has changed noticeably relative to that during its baseline period. Therefore we also included, as independent variables in the regression, a second-order Fourier series with period 1 yr, in order to compensate for a residual annual cycle in the data.
We also tested the sensitivity of our results to the choice of data used to represent the exogenous factors. In addition to MEI we used the southern oscillation index (SOI) to represent ENSO, we replaced the Sato et al aerosol data with that of Ammann et al (2003), and in place of TSI we used sunspot numbers as an indicator of solar activity. None of these substitutions affected the results in a significant way, so we report the results using MEI, Sato et al AOD data and TSI.
Because the exogenous factors themselves may show correlation with each other (for instance, TSI and AOD both show a peak around 1982), we tested for the impact of collinearity in the regression by computing results when each of the exogenous factors is omitted from the analysis, as well as by computing the condition number of the design matrix. The condition number is modest, and omitting individual factors leads to only small changes in the coefficients for the remaining factors, indicating that collinearity is not a significant impediment to the multiple regression.

3. RESULTS
The raw warming rates for the five data records, i.e., the linear trend rates computed directly from the unadjusted data, are listed in table 1, together with the trend rates after adjustment for exogenous factors, and the estimated lags. Standard errors for the trend rates take into account the autocorrelation of the residuals (see the appendix). All five records show highly statistically significant warming, at rates ranging from 0.014 K yr-1 (UAH) to 0.018 K yr-1 (GISS).
The impact of the exogenous factors is estimated by their regression coefficients. The coefficients of temperature response to MEI, AOD and TSI are plotted in figure 3, with 2-sigma error bars. The LT records show a markedly stronger response to both the MEI and to AOD than do the surface records. The response to solar variations is less pronounced, and the difference between LT and surface response is not statistically significant.
When the estimated influence of the exogenous factors is removed from the data, the adjusted data sets show noticeably less short-term variation than the raw data. The adjusted data sets are plotted in figure 4. Their consistency is impressive; clearly the five records reflect the same essential changes in the global temperature. This is even clearer in the graph of annual averages of adjusted data in figure 5. It is worthy of note that for all five adjusted data sets, 2009 and 2010 are the two hottest years on record.
The adjusted data enable a more precise evaluation of the warming rates, as evidenced by the reduction in the probable errors of the trend rates from the adjusted data compared to the raw data (table 1). For the surface temperature records the full range of trend rates is from 0.014 to 0.018 K yr-1, but for the adjusted data the range is only 0.0154-0.0175 K yr-1.

4. IS GLOBAL WARMING SLOWING DOWN, OR ACCELERATING?
It has been suggested (e.g., Easterling and Wehner 2009, and references therein) that the rate of global warming has lessened, or even that global warming has stopped during the last decade or so. Most such claims are based on examining the warming rate over brief time intervals, an approach which even with adjusted data is subject to great uncertainty because of the brevity of the time span, and with unadjusted data is often profoundly influenced by the exaggerated impact of short-term fluctuations due to exogenous factors, especially at the endpoints of the time interval. To illustrate the impact of the choice of starting year on estimates of the recent warming rate, we computed the trend rate for each adjusted data set, from various starting years (from 1979 through 2005) to the present. The results (figure 6) show that for all five adjusted data sets, the estimated warming rate does not show any sign of decrease, no matter what the starting year, within the estimated (2-sigma) uncertainty of the trend estimation. Hence it becomes obvious that there has not been any cessation, or even any slowing, of global warming over the last decade.
We also tested for any change in the global warming rate by fitting a quadratic function of time to the adjusted data. For only one of the data sets, UAH, does the quadratic term make a statistically significant contribution (p = 0.03), and its sign indicates acceleration of the warming rate rather than deceleration. However, we regard this acceleration with skepticism because it shows in no other data set, not even the other satellite record.

6. CONCLUSIONS
This analysis confirms the strong influence of known factors on short-term variations in global temperature, including ENSO, volcanic aerosols and to a lesser degree solar variation. It also emphasizes that LT temperature is affected by these factors much more strongly than surface temperature. Perhaps most important, it enables us to remove an estimate of their influence, thereby isolating the global warming signal. The resultant adjusted data show clearly, both visually and when subjected to statistical analysis, that the rate of global warming due to other factors (most likely these are exclusively anthropogenic) has been remarkably steady during the 32 years from 1979 through 2010. There is no indication of any slowdown or acceleration of global warming, beyond the variability induced by these known natural factors. Because the effects of volcanic eruptions and of ENSO are very short-term and that of solar variability very small (figure 7), none of these factors can be expected to exert a significant influence on the continuation of global warming over the coming decades. The close agreement between all five adjusted data sets suggests that it is meaningful to average them in order to produce a composite record of planetary warming. Annual averages of the result are shown in figure 8. This is the true global warming signal. Its unabated increase is powerful evidence that we can expect further temperature increase in the next few decades, emphasizing the urgency of confronting the human influence on climate.
